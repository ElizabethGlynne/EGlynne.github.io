My Teaching Philosophy

I teach biology to help students understand the living world as a network of interconnected, dynamic systems—and to develop the habits of mind needed to investigate it.

My guiding question, especially in introductory biology, is: How does life work, persist, and change? It invites students to connect biological mechanisms with continuity, diversity, and change across scales.

Concepts Before Isolated Facts

I want students to move beyond recognizing terms toward explaining relationships, predicting outcomes, and applying ideas to unfamiliar situations. Evolutionary thinking offers a powerful organizing perspective, but the central questions and explanatory models should fit the course being taught.

Research on learning emphasizes building connected knowledge and supporting transfer across contexts (National Academies, 2018).

Learning Through Evidence and Participation

Scientific understanding develops through questions, models, observations, data, and revision. I use laboratory investigations, guided practice, discussion, authentic datasets, and computational exploration where they advance the learning objectives.

Meta-analyses in undergraduate STEM support well-designed active learning, while also showing that implementation matters (Freeman et al., 2014; Theobald et al., 2020).

Inclusive Access, Meaningful Expectations

Students arrive with different experiences, strengths, and ways of engaging with science. I aim to offer clear objectives, multiple opportunities for practice and feedback, and varied ways to participate without abandoning the scientific understanding each course requires.

Intentional classroom structure can broaden participation and foster equity (Tanner, 2013).

Models, Technology, and Scientific Computing

Visual models, simulations, R-based analyses, and digital anatomy can make complex systems more visible and investigable. I use these as tools for reasoning—not as substitutes for understanding biological assumptions, limitations, or evidence.

Students should be able to explain what a model represents, what it leaves out, and what conclusions its results support.

One Philosophy, Different Courses

My philosophy is a set of commitments, not a fixed teaching recipe. A face-to-face laboratory, an asynchronous biology course, and a scientific computing course need not use identical activities or assessments. Their learning outcomes, students, delivery constraints, and disciplinary practices should determine the methods.

For example, introductory biology may emphasize explaining mechanisms through experiments and comparative examples; an online course may use structured case analysis and feedback; scientific computing may center reproducible notebooks and model critique. What remains consistent is the emphasis on conceptual coherence, evidence, transparent expectations, and opportunities to revise understanding.

Reflective, Evidence-Informed Course Design

I treat teaching as an iterative practice. I begin with what students should be able to explain or do, align practice and assessment with those goals, examine where students struggle, and revise materials accordingly. Educational research informs my decisions, but I also evaluate whether an approach is appropriate for a particular course and group of learners.

My aim is for students to leave with more than course-specific facts: the ability to ask better questions, evaluate evidence, make connections, and approach unfamiliar biological problems with curiosity.
