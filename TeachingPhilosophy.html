Teaching • Learning • Scientific Inquiry

My Teaching Philosophy

I want students to discover that science is something they can participate in—not simply a collection of answers they are expected to remember.

What draws me to biology is that every discovery reveals new connections and often raises questions we had not thought to ask. I want students to experience that curiosity, to see relationships they had not noticed before, and to develop the confidence to investigate what they do not yet understand.

Why I Teach

I teach because I want students to look at the world differently. I want them to recognize patterns, ask why things happen, and understand that scientific explanations are built from evidence—and can change when new evidence emerges.

My aim is not for every student to become a biologist. It is for students to leave more capable of asking questions, evaluating claims, making connections, and approaching unfamiliar problems with curiosity.

Curiosity

Questions are the starting point of understanding. I invite students to make predictions, test ideas, and remain open to being surprised by what they discover.

Connection

Biology is not a collection of unrelated vocabulary. I help students connect structures, processes, organisms, environments, and evolutionary change, and transfer those connections to new problems.

Research on learning emphasizes connected knowledge and transfer (National Academies, 2018).

Discovery Through Evidence

Students should have opportunities to participate in scientific reasoning: observing, analyzing data, building and critiquing models, discussing explanations, and revising ideas. Well-designed active learning can support performance and narrow achievement gaps, though its effectiveness depends on how it is implemented (Freeman et al., 2014; Theobald et al., 2020).

What This Looks Like in My Classroom

I use laboratory investigations, guided practice, comparative examples, authentic datasets, visual explanations, and computational tools when they help students meet a learning goal. I want students to explain not only what a result shows, but how they know—and what remains uncertain.

I also recognize that students bring different experiences, strengths, and needs. I work to make expectations clear, provide meaningful practice and feedback, and create multiple ways to participate without lowering the scientific goals of the course. Deliberate classroom structures can help broaden participation (Tanner, 2013).

Technology is a means, not an end: an R simulation or a digital anatomical model is useful when students can examine its assumptions, limitations, and evidence.

Different Courses, Shared Commitments

My teaching philosophy is not a fixed recipe. A face-to-face biology laboratory, an asynchronous course, and a scientific computing course call for different activities, supports, and assessments. I choose methods based on the students, disciplinary questions, and what they should be able to explain or do.

Across those settings, I return to the same commitments: curiosity, conceptual understanding, evidence, accessible participation, and meaningful expectations. Each course can have its own organizing questions; the question “How does life work, persist, and change?” belongs to my introductory biology course rather than serving as a universal teaching slogan.

Learning Alongside My Students

I see teaching as an iterative practice. I start with learning objectives, align practice and assessment, look carefully at where students struggle, and revise my approach. Educational research informs those choices, but the needs of a particular course and its learners matter too.
